Open standards body

Defining and verifying post-quantum readiness for blockchain.

The Quantum Alliance sets the criteria for judging quantum-resistance claims in blockchain and creates standards for compatibility between networks as they migrate. It defines what post-quantum readiness means for networks, wallets, and infrastructure, and how to test and verify any claim of quantum resistance.

The threat

Why now

Nearly every major blockchain secures its accounts with elliptic-curve signatures. Once an account signs a transaction (for some address types, from the moment it is created), its public key sits permanently on a public ledger. A sufficiently capable quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could work backward from that public key to the private key and take control of the funds.

$2.9T

Approximate crypto market value that still relies on classical signatures.

6.04M BTC

Bitcoin counted as quantum-exposed at rest: 30.2% of supply.

<500k

Physical qubits that may be enough to break the elliptic-curve cryptography behind most major coins, about a twentieth of earlier estimates.

The same signatures protect stablecoin minting keys, governance contracts, bridges, and oracle feeds, so one compromised privileged key could spread across many platforms at once. A growing body of forecasts places the first practical attack around 2028.

Aug 2024

NIST finalizes its first post-quantum cryptography standards. No single party can order a live blockchain to adopt them.

Early 2025

The Quantum Doom Clock, a model built by Alliance contributors Richard T. Carback III and Colton Dillion, points to March 2028 as an early window for a cryptographically relevant quantum computer.

Nov 2025

Scott Aaronson writes that a fault-tolerant machine running Shor's algorithm before the next US presidential election has become a live possibility.

Mar 2026

Google Quantum AI, with researchers from the Ethereum Foundation and Stanford, finds that cracking the elliptic-curve cryptography behind Bitcoin, Ether, and most major coins could take fewer than 500,000 physical qubits.

Apr 2026

Aaronson relays the view of leading hardware experts that a machine able to break deployed cryptosystems could arrive around 2029: "This post is your warning."

Sep 2026

IonQ estimates that a fault-tolerant trapped-ion computer with roughly 20,000 physical qubits could solve Bitcoin's secp256k1 problem in about 26 days per attempt. No existing machine can do this yet.

No shared yardstick

The industry has never agreed on what "quantum-ready" means for a network or how anyone should check it. A May analysis of the qLABS Quantum Vulnerability Index ranked Ethereum second among prepared networks, citing its roadmap and active research. The migration.fail assessment, a scorecard that counts only cryptography already live, gives Ethereum an F. Each method can be defended on its own terms, and neither gives exchanges, custodians, developers, or asset holders a common reference point. The Quantum Alliance was formed to set that bar.

First body of work

What the Alliance will publish

Everything the Alliance produces is published openly, so projects can adopt it directly and anyone can measure a readiness claim against the same yardstick.

01

A public definition of quantum readiness

Covering transaction signatures, key management, consensus, zero-knowledge proof systems, and peer-to-peer connections.

02

Open test criteria and benchmarks

Tests that any team can run against its own implementation.

03

Reference designs for migration

Designs that address exposed keys, dormant accounts, and the larger transactions post-quantum signatures produce.

Charter

Principles

The Alliance is modeled on the Internet Engineering Task Force. Proposals advance on public evidence, rough consensus, and working implementations. Corporate size and token holdings carry no voting weight. AI-moderated tooling keeps the process legible at scale.

Open participation

There are no dues, no applications, and no prerequisites. Anyone can contribute.

Individuals, not companies

Contributions are judged on merit. Affiliation is disclosed, not weighted.

Rough consensus and working code

No voting. Chairs assess the strength of arguments. Implementations carry more weight than theory.

Evidence always

No overstating quantum readiness. No fear-based marketing.

Technical work

Working groups

Technical work happens in four working groups. Each has a charter, defined deliverables, and one or two chairs.

WG 1

PQC Migration

Standards for transitioning existing chains.

How live networks can move funds and keys to post-quantum schemes, including accounts whose public keys are already exposed and coins whose owners have lost access. StarkWare's quantum-safe Bitcoin transaction, mined on mainnet in August 2026, shows both the progress made and the distance remaining. The Alliance's criteria make the gap between a single working demonstration and network-wide readiness visible to everyone.

WG 2

ZK-PQ

Post-quantum zero-knowledge implementations.

Privacy networks, rollups, and many cross-chain tools depend on proof systems. Systems built on pairing-friendly elliptic curves carry the same quantum weakness as today's signatures. Hash-based designs such as zk-STARKs avoid those assumptions but produce larger proofs. The group defines how post-quantum proof systems are evaluated and benchmarked.

WG 3

Wallet Security

Hardware and software wallet PQC standards.

How users and custodians generate, store, and sign with post-quantum keys, from hardware secure elements to recovery flows. The group builds on existing efforts such as Wallet Security for Post-Quantum Ethereum, an initiative co-authored by Dr. Carback and listed with TheDAO Security Fund.

WG 4

Interoperability

Cross-chain PQC compatibility.

Interoperability specifications that let wallets, bridges, and hardware signers handle post-quantum keys consistently across chains. A transfer that crosses several networks inherits the weakest protection among them, so shared rules at these junctions carry as much weight as upgrades to any single chain.

"Zero-knowledge systems carry some of the most sensitive data on any chain, and their security assumptions have to survive the same transition as signatures. Agreeing on how to evaluate post-quantum proof systems now saves every team from solving it alone later."

Sebastien Guillemot, CTO, Midnight Foundation

"Hardware wallets sit at the point where new cryptography meets real users. Common specifications let device makers support post-quantum keys once and have them work everywhere."

Aaron Chen, CTO, Keystone

Chairs

One or two per working group, confirmed by the Steering Committee. Chairs facilitate and assess consensus. They do not decide outcomes, and may not act as the deciding voice on their own submissions.

Discussion tooling

Working groups run as a structured debate tree, not a mailing list. Each open question is a node; contributions attach for or against, with evidence. An AI layer removes duplicates, summarizes each branch, surfaces the unresolved crux, and flags convergence. It keeps the record legible but does not decide: chairs assess consensus, members ratify. At Last Call the chair works from the branch summary, with open objections and agreed points laid out and auditable. Converged nodes feed the quarterly report and IETF submissions. The tool and its full record are public; the convening organization provides it as administration only. Until that tool is public, contribution happens on GitHub.

Rough consensus

A chair declares consensus by issuing a two-week Last Call on the working group channel. If no unresolved objection remains at close, the document advances. The test is that most participants agree and no strong objection stands, not a headcount.

Deadlock

If a working group cannot resolve a substantive objection, either side may escalate to the Steering Committee, which rules on process only, not on technical merits. Appeals must state numbered, specific objections. The Committee may uphold the chair's call, return the item with instructions (a narrower scope is common), or drop it. An item that cannot reach consensus returns to Discussion Paper status and is subject to the standard 6-month expiry. Where a working group has only two active participants, unanimity is required.

New working groups

Used only when a topic fits no existing working group. A new working group requires:

  1. A written charter
  2. Three or more participants
  3. Steering Committee approval

Maturity

Standards documents

Three maturity levels. All documents are public.

TypeWhat it is
Discussion Paper (DP)Working proposal. Anyone can submit. Expires in 6 months if not advanced.
Proposed Standard (PS)Achieved rough consensus in its working group and Steering Committee review. Stable for implementation.
Alliance Standard (AS)Proven implementation across 2+ independent projects. Highest maturity.

Submitting a Discussion Paper

Anyone may submit a Discussion Paper, and all submissions are posted publicly on the working group list immediately. Nothing is silently rejected. To be scheduled for discussion on a working group call, a paper must (1) fall within the working group's charter scope and (2) have at least one supporter other than the author. Chairs schedule on those two criteria alone, not on their view of the idea. A paper unsupported after 6 months expires quietly and may be resubmitted. An author may ask the Steering Committee to review any scheduling decision.

Terms

Intellectual property and licensing

Scope

These terms apply only to contributions made into Alliance work items (documents, standards, reports). Joining the Alliance grants no license over a member's pre-existing, patented, or unrelated IP. A member's portfolio is untouched unless they choose to contribute it into a work item.

Copyright

Contributors retain copyright in their contributions and grant the Alliance a perpetual, irrevocable, royalty-free license to publish, modify, and create derivative works from them.

Standards

Final Alliance standards and reports are published under CC BY 4.0: freely accessible to anyone, no paywall, no membership requirement. The license covers the document text, not the underlying technology.

Implementations

Reference implementations may carry their own licenses, provided they remain free and open for free and open derivative works.

Patents

Contributors must disclose any known essential IP (patents or pending applications) that read on a work item they participate in. Claims essential to implementing an Alliance standard must be licensed to implementers of that standard on royalty-free or RAND terms; the commitment covers implementation of the standard only and does not open-license the patent for any other use. Undisclosed essential IP disqualifies the affected contribution from standardization. Members who prefer to keep a patent fully proprietary simply keep it out of contributed standards.

Process

Governance

Steering Committee

Five to seven individuals. Approves new working groups, signs off on document advancement (PS and AS), publishes quarterly reports, and hears appeals. It does not author standards.

Composition

Founding members seed the initial Committee, one individual per founding member, up to the 5–7 cap. Individuals, not companies, hold seats; no organization holds more than one seat. The Committee is not limited to founding members beyond the initial term.

Elections

From the first term rotation, seats are filled by election every year. Eligible voters and candidates: authors of Alliance work items in the preceding 5 years, individuals with recorded contributions in the current period, current or former working group chairs, and founding members for the first 5 years. Committee structure and eligibility rules are amendable by supermajority (two-thirds) of eligible voters.

Neutrality

The Committee carries no company's branding, name, or logo. The convening organization provides administration only and holds no vote or veto. No organization chairs the Committee for two terms running.

Terms

Seats run 12 months, then rotate or renew. If founding members exceed the cap, seats rotate annually so every member cycles through.

Participation

Join any working group channel or attend an assembly. Optionally register name and affiliation.

Conflicts of interest

Participants must disclose any affiliation with, or funding from, an organization whose mission or goals materially conflict with the Alliance's mission. The test is the organization's mandate, not its sector: an agency tasked with building interoperable standards is not a conflict; an agency whose mandate includes compromising cryptography is, as is any group dedicated to banning cryptocurrency. Disclosed conflicts do not bar participation in open discussion, but individuals with an active conflict may not hold leadership roles (working group chair or Steering Committee seat) until at least 3 years after the affiliation or funding has ended. The Steering Committee may impose further limitations where needed and hears appeals from any affected individual.

Removal

For sustained violation of Alliance principles or material non-disclosure of a conflict, including conduct discovered after the fact. Decided by the Steering Committee.

Confidentiality

Working group discussions, minutes, and records are public by default. A session may reserve time at its end for off-the-record discussion under the Chatham House Rule, declared explicitly on entry. Members may share confidential or unreleased work in these slots or via privately distributed materials. Nothing shared off the record enters a public document without the owner's written consent.
